Unit 4: Electromagnetic Induction

The complete, unsummarized lecture notes for PHY 102 Unit 4, extracted directly from the official Comprehensive Handbook.

4.0 Electromagnetic Induction

In 1831, Michael Faraday discovered that an electric current can be produced in a conductor by a changing magnetic field. The current produced is called an induced current, and the phenomenon is called electromagnetic induction. Induction occurs whenever there is relative motion between a conductor and a magnetic field, or whenever the magnetic flux through a circuit changes.

4.1 Faraday’s Laws of Electromagnetic Induction

First Law: Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an EMF is induced in the circuit.

Second Law: The magnitude of the induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux.

ℰ = −N (dΦ / dt)    |    Φ = B A cosθ

More turns (N) means more flux is linked, so larger EMF. Faster change (larger dΦ/dt) produces larger EMF. The negative sign is Lenz’s law. Magnetic flux Φ is maximum when the field is perpendicular to the coil (θ = 0°) and zero when the field is parallel to the coil (θ = 90°).

4.2 Lenz’s Law

Lenz’s law states that the induced EMF (and the resulting current) is always in such a direction as to oppose the change that produced it. The negative sign in Faraday’s law is the mathematical expression of Lenz’s law. It ensures conservation of energy — the induced current never helps the change that created it.

★ MUST KNOW: If you push a north pole of a magnet towards a coil, the induced current creates a north pole facing the approaching magnet (repulsion). If you pull the north pole away, the induced current creates a south pole (attraction). Always oppose the motion.

calculate Worked Example: Induced EMF

Problem: A coil of 50 turns is removed in 0.02 s from a magnetic field. The flux changes from 3.1 × 10⁻⁴ Wb to 0.10 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. Find the average induced EMF.

Solution:
∆Φ = |0.10 × 10⁻⁴ − 3.1 × 10⁻⁴| = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb
ℰ = N (∆Φ / ∆t) = 50 × (3.0 × 10⁻⁴) / 0.02 = 0.75 V

4.3 Self-Inductance

Self-inductance is the property of a coil by which it opposes any change in the current flowing through it by inducing an EMF in itself. A coil has a self-inductance of 1 henry (H) if a current changing at 1 A/s induces an EMF of 1 V in the coil. Larger L means the coil more strongly resists changes in current.

ℰ = −L (dI / dt)    |    L = N Φ / I

4.4 Mutual Inductance

Mutual inductance is the property by which a change of current in one coil (primary) induces an EMF in a nearby second coil (secondary).

ℰ_s = −M (dI_p / dt)    |    M = N_s Φ_s / I_p

calculate Worked Example: Mutual Inductance

Problem: A current of 5 A in the primary produces a flux of 0.004 Wb in each of 100 turns of the secondary. Find M.

Solution:
M = (N_s × Φ_s) / I_p = (100 × 0.004) / 5 = 0.08 H

4.5 Transformers

A transformer changes the size of an alternating voltage using the principle of mutual induction. It consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound on a soft-iron core.

E_s / E_p = N_s / N_p = I_p / I_s
  • Step-up: N_s > N_p → secondary voltage higher, secondary current lower.
  • Step-down: N_s < N_p → secondary voltage lower, secondary current higher.

Efficiency: η = (E_s I_s / E_p I_p) × 100%

calculate Worked Example: Transformer Efficiency

Problem: A transformer produces 8 V from a 240 V AC supply with 80% efficiency. If the secondary current is 15 A, find the primary current.

Solution:
0.80 = (8 × 15) / (240 × I_p)
0.80 × 240 × I_p = 120
I_p = 120 / 192 = 0.625 A

4.6 Eddy Currents

Eddy currents are circulating currents induced in bulk conductors by a changing magnetic field. They waste energy as heat. Transformer cores are laminated (made of thin insulated sheets) to break up the eddy-current paths and reduce losses.

Factors that increase induced EMF: (1) More turns N; (2) Stronger magnetic field or larger area; (3) Faster rate of change of flux; (4) Higher permeability of the core.
