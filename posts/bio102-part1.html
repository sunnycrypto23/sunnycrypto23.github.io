📘 Module 1: Viruses

The Context: What Makes a Virus Unique?

Viruses are genetic material (DNA or RNA, never both) enclosed in a protective protein coat called the capsid. A virus is not a cell — it has no membrane-bound organelles, no cytoplasm of its own, and no independent metabolism.

Analogy: A virus is like a piece of software code. By itself, it can't do anything — it just sits there. But when it gets into a computer (a host cell), it hijacks the system to replicate itself. The virus isn't alive, but it can cause living things to make more of it.

Living or non-living? Viruses sit in a gray zone. They fail most tests of life (cannot use energy, respond to stimuli, grow, or reproduce independently) but they can evolve, since their genetic material mutates over time — a genuinely lifelike trait. This is why viruses cannot be placed into any of the three domains of life (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya): those domains classify cells, and a virus is not a cell.

1.1 Basic Characteristics

  • A virus is nothing more than DNA or RNA surrounded by a protein coat.
  • A virus cannot use energy, respond to stimuli, grow, or maintain homeostasis.
  • A virus cannot reproduce on its own — it must infect a host cell and hijack its machinery.
  • Because it has genetic material and can reproduce (via a host), a virus can evolve.

1.2 Structure

Viruses range from about 20–250 nm in size (far smaller than bacteria; an electron microscope is needed to see them). Key structural terms:

  • Capsid — the protective protein coat surrounding the genome.
  • Nucleocapsid — the capsid plus the enclosed genetic material together.
  • Envelope — an outer lipid membrane some viruses acquire from the host cell (e.g., HIV); not all viruses have one.
  • Virion — a complete, infectious virus particle: genome + capsid (+ envelope if present). This is the whole assembled unit.

Complexity varies hugely: the simplest viruses encode around 4 proteins; the most complex encode 100–200 proteins.

1.3 Classification of Viruses

1.4 Identification of Viruses

Once a virus infects a host, embryo, or cell culture, samples are examined under brightfield, electron, or fluorescent microscopy. For plant viruses specifically, identification methods include:

  • Inoculating indicator plants and observing symptoms
  • Serological tests using antisera of known viruses
  • Studying transmission aspects (sap inoculation, insect vectors, persistence in vector)
  • Measuring thermal inactivation point, dilution end point, survival outside the host
  • Testing interaction with other viruses (cross-protection)

1.5 Cultivation of Viruses

Viruses cannot grow on standard broth/agar media — they need a living host. Methods include:

  • Culturing in bacteria — bacteria + phages mixed on agar; infected cells lyse and release new phages, producing clear zones called plaques; each plaque = one original phage (the basis of the plaque assay).
  • Cell/tissue culture — animal cells grown as a monolayer, then inoculated; plaques or cytopathic effects indicate viral growth.
  • Whole organs/animals — monkeys, rabbits, guinea pigs, mice used historically (e.g., yellow fever research).
  • Embryonated chicken eggs — inexpensive; virus injected into the chorioallantoic membrane or allantoic cavity depending on which the virus prefers.
  • Plant tissue/whole plants — leaves mechanically inoculated with an abrasive, or via insect vectors, or by grafting a diseased part onto a healthy plant.

📝 Module 1 Self-Assessment Test

Q1: Why can't viruses be classified into any of the three domains of life?
Q2: Is a virus alive? Explain.
Q3: Define capsid, nucleocapsid, and virion, and explain how they differ.
Q4: List the five bases used to classify viruses.
Q5: Explain why Rabies virus can correctly be described as both an 'animal virus' and 'zoonotic'.
Q6: What is a plaque, and how is it used to estimate phage numbers?
Q7: Name three methods used to cultivate viruses in the lab.

✅ Full Solutions

A1: Because the domains (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya) classify cells, and a virus is not a cell — it has no plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, or independent metabolism of its own.

A2: It's genuinely ambiguous. Viruses fail most tests of life (no independent metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, or self-reproduction) but they can evolve, since their DNA/RNA mutates over time — a lifelike trait. Most biologists describe them as existing in a gray zone between living and non-living.

A3: The capsid is the protein coat around the genetic material. The nucleocapsid is the capsid plus the enclosed genome together. The virion is the complete infectious particle — genome + capsid (and envelope, if present) — i.e., the whole assembled virus.

A4: (1) Type of nucleic acid, (2) structure/symmetry, (3) site of replication, (4) host range, (5) mode of transmission.

A5: These come from two different, independent classification axes. 'Animal virus' classifies Rabies by host range (it infects animals). 'Zoonotic' classifies it by mode of transmission (it spreads via the bite of an infected animal). A virus can sit in one category from each axis simultaneously.

A6: A plaque is a clear zone in an otherwise uniform bacterial lawn on an agar plate, caused by bacteriophages lysing infected bacterial cells. Because each plaque originates from a single phage, counting plaques gives an estimate of the number of infectious phage particles in the original sample.

A7: (1) Embryonated chicken eggs — e.g., mumps virus grown in the allantoic cavity. (2) Cell/tissue culture — animal cells grown as a monolayer. (3) Culturing in bacteria — bacteriophages grown on bacterial lawns, producing plaques.

📘 Module 2: Bacteria

The Context: The Most Abundant Life Forms on Earth

Bacteria are simple, single-celled prokaryotic organisms — no true nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles. They are found everywhere: in soil, water, extreme environments (extremophiles), and living organisms. They are essential for nutrient cycling, decomposition, and many industrial processes, but some also cause disease.

Analogy: Bacteria are like tiny factories. Each one is a self-contained unit with its own DNA (the blueprint), ribosomes (the assembly line), and a cell wall (the building structure). They can replicate rapidly, adapt to new environments, and even share genetic material with each other like factories sharing blueprints.

2.1 General Characteristics

  • Simple, single-celled prokaryotic organisms — no true nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles.
  • Size: roughly 0.5–1.0 μm in diameter.
  • DNA is free-floating in a region called the nucleoid; additional small circular DNA molecules are called plasmids (often carry antibiotic resistance genes).
  • Cell wall contains peptidoglycan, giving the cell its shape and rigidity.
  • Reproduce asexually by binary fission, but gain genetic diversity through transformation, transduction, and conjugation.
  • Ubiquitous — found in soil, water, extreme environments, and living organisms.

2.4 Gram Staining

The main technique for a first-pass classification of bacteria, using crystal violet dye, iodine, and a safranin counterstain.

TypeResultWhy
Gram-positiveStains violetThick peptidoglycan wall traps the violet-iodine complex
Gram-negativeStains redThin wall + outer lipopolysaccharide layer loses the violet-iodine complex, retain safranin
Note: Gram staining tells you general group, not exact species — it's a screening tool, not a definitive ID method.

📝 Module 2 Self-Assessment Test

Q1: What structural feature is exclusively found in bacterial cell walls, and why does it matter?
Q2: Explain the mechanism behind why Gram-positive bacteria stain violet and Gram-negative bacteria stain red.
Q3: Distinguish between the nucleoid and a plasmid in a bacterial cell.
Q4: List the three main bacterial shapes and give one named example of each.
Q5: Name two ways bacteria can exchange genetic material besides binary fission.

✅ Full Solutions

A1: Peptidoglycan. It matters because it's the target of antibiotics like penicillin, and its presence/composition is the basis of Gram staining, a key first-pass identification method.

A2: Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan wall that traps the crystal violet-iodine complex, so they retain the violet stain. Gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer plus an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane; this structure loses the violet-iodine complex during the decolorizing step and instead retains the safranin counterstain, appearing red.

A3: The nucleoid is the region containing the bacterium's main circular chromosome, free-floating in the cytoplasm (not membrane-bound). A plasmid is a separate, smaller circular DNA molecule, additional to the main chromosome, that often carries genes such as antibiotic resistance and can be transferred between bacteria (e.g., via conjugation).

A4: Coccus (spherical) — Streptococcus pneumoniae. Bacillus (rod-shaped) — E. coli. Spiral — Spirillum volutans (rigid) or Vibrio cholerae (comma-shaped, a variant).

A5: Transformation (absorbing free DNA released by other bacteria into the environment) and conjugation (direct DNA transfer between two bacteria via a pilus). A third method is transduction, where a bacteriophage transfers DNA from one bacterium to another.
