📘 Module 3: Fungi

The Context: The Great Decomposers

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms — they have a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (but no chloroplasts). They are heterotrophic — they obtain nutrients by decomposing organic matter or via symbiosis. Their cell walls are made of chitin (the same material as insect exoskeletons), not cellulose. They reproduce via spores and are non-motile (with rare flagellated exceptions in primitive groups).

Analogy: Fungi are like nature's recyclers. They break down dead organic matter — fallen trees, dead animals, leaves — and return nutrients to the soil. Without fungi, ecosystems would be buried in dead material with no way to recycle nutrients back into the food chain.

3.1 Basic Characteristics

  • Eukaryotic — true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (no chloroplasts, though).
  • Heterotrophic — obtain nutrients by decomposing organic matter or via symbiosis.
  • Cell walls made of chitin (same material as insect exoskeletons), not cellulose.
  • Reproduce via spores.
  • Non-motile (with rare flagellated exceptions in primitive groups).
  • Body = thallus; thread-like filaments = hyphae; a network of hyphae = mycelium.
  • Important decomposers and nutrient recyclers; also form symbioses (mycorrhizae with plant roots, lichens with algae/cyanobacteria).
  • Produce secondary metabolites: antibiotics, toxins, pigments.

3.2 Nutrition — Digestion Before Ingestion

Unlike animals, fungi digest outside the body first: they secrete exoenzymes that break down complex organic material (e.g., cellulose, lignin) into small molecules, which are then absorbed across the hyphal surface. Most fungi are saprobes — living off dead organic matter.

Key Insight: This is the reverse of animal digestion. Animals ingest first and digest internally. Fungi digest externally and then absorb. This is why you see mold growing on bread — it's digesting the bread from the outside in.

3.3 Identification

  • Morphology — shape and size (mushroom, mold, yeast form)
  • Spore print — color and shape of spores
  • Chemical tests — e.g., the KOH test

📝 Module 3 Self-Assessment Test

Q1: Explain what is meant by fungi practicing 'digestion before ingestion.'
Q2: What is the fungal cell wall made of, and what other organism group shares this material?
Q3: Define thallus, hypha, and mycelium.
Q4: Match each fungal group to its common name and one example: Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Zygomycota, Chytridiomycota, Deuteromycota.

✅ Full Solutions

A1: Fungi secrete exoenzymes outward into their environment, breaking down complex organic material into small molecules externally, before absorbing those smaller molecules across the hyphal surface. This is the reverse order of digestion in animals, which ingest first and digest internally.

A2: Chitin — the same structural material found in the exoskeleton of arthropods (insects, crustaceans, etc.).

A3: Thallus is the overall body/vegetative structure of a fungus. A hypha is a single thread-like filament. Mycelium is a network made up of many hyphae together.

A4: Ascomycota = sac fungi (morels, truffles, cup fungi). Basidiomycota = club fungi (mushrooms, puffballs, bracket fungi). Zygomycota = conjugated fungi (bread mold, Rhizopus). Chytridiomycota = chytrid fungi (aquatic decomposers). Deuteromycota = imperfect fungi (no known spore stage — many yeasts and molds).

📘 Module 4: Ecological Adaptation in Plants

The Context: The Great Colonization of Land

Plants evolved from green algae and made the transition from water to land. This required major innovations to solve the problems of desiccation (drying out), gravity (needing structural support), and nutrient transport (moving water and food over long distances).

Analogy: Moving from water to land was like moving from living in a swimming pool to living in a desert. You need completely different equipment: a way to hold water, a way to stand up, and a way to move resources around. Plants evolved all of this over millions of years.

4.1 Vascular Tissue — The Key Innovation

Xylem (built from lignin-strengthened tracheid cells) transports water upward from roots to leaves. Phloem transports sugars from source (site of production, e.g., leaves) to sink (site of use, e.g., roots, fruits). This let plants transport water and food over greater distances, enabling larger body size.

Why It Matters: Without vascular tissue, plants could only grow close to the ground in wet environments. Vascular tissue is what allowed plants to grow tall, compete for sunlight, and colonize diverse terrestrial habitats.

4.2 True Roots, Stems, and Leaves

Vascular tissue allowed the evolution of:

  • True roots — deeper anchorage, nutrient uptake, often form mycorrhizal partnerships with fungi
  • Stems — centralize the plant body, allow height, support leaves
  • Leaves — increase surface area for photosynthesis

Microphylls — single-veined, earliest leaf-like structures (lycophytes, e.g., club mosses).

Euphylls — true leaves with multiple veins and photosynthetic tissue between veins (ferns, horsetails, and most other vascular plants).

4.3 Dominant Sporophyte Generation

Unlike non-vascular plants, seedless vascular plants have a dominant, independent diploid sporophyte generation. The haploid gametophyte generation still exists but is reduced and independent.

Example: In a fern, the large leafy plant you see is the diploid sporophyte. The small, heart-shaped gametophyte grows nearby but is much smaller and less conspicuous. In non-vascular plants like mosses, the gametophyte is the dominant green structure, and the sporophyte is smaller and dependent on it.

📝 Module 4 Self-Assessment Test

Q1: What is the single most important innovation that allowed seedless vascular plants to grow larger and colonize new terrain, and what are its two tissue types?
Q2: Distinguish microphylls from euphylls.
Q3: What generation is dominant in seedless vascular plants, and how does this differ from non-vascular plants?

✅ Full Solutions

A1: Vascular tissue. Its two types are xylem (transports water, strengthened by lignin-containing tracheid cells) and phloem (transports sugars from source to sink).

A2: Microphylls are single-veined, structurally simple leaf-like structures found in lycophytes (e.g., club mosses) — the earliest leaf-like structures in vascular plants. Euphylls are true leaves with multiple veins and photosynthetic tissue between the veins, found in ferns, horsetails, and most other vascular plants.

A3: The diploid sporophyte generation is dominant and grows independently. In non-vascular plants, the haploid gametophyte generation is dominant, and the sporophyte is smaller and dependent on it.
