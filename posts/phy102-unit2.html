Unit 2: DC Circuits & Components

The complete, unsummarized lecture notes for PHY 102 Unit 2, extracted directly from the official Comprehensive Handbook.

2.0 The Electric Circuit

An electric circuit is a closed path through which electric charge can flow continuously. Every complete circuit must contain:

  • A power source (battery, generator) — supplies energy.
  • Conducting path (wires) — low-resistance route for charge.
  • A load (resistor, lamp, motor) — converts electrical energy into another form.
  • Optionally a switch — opens or closes the path.

Key Quantities: Voltage V (potential difference, unit volt); Current I = Q/t (rate of flow of charge, unit ampere); Resistance R (opposition to current flow, unit ohm).

2.1 Ohm’s Law

Ohm’s law states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance (provided temperature remains constant).

V = I R

A bigger voltage pushes more charge per second (higher current). A bigger resistance makes it harder for charge to flow, so current decreases. The product V = IR captures both effects.

2.2 Resistance and Resistivity

R = ρ L / A

Longer wire (larger L) → more collisions → higher R. Thicker wire (larger A) → more paths for electrons → lower R. ρ (resistivity) is a property of the material only.

Factors affecting Resistance: (1) Length L — R ∝ L; (2) Cross-sectional area A — R ∝ 1/A; (3) Material (resistivity ρ); (4) Temperature — for metals, R increases with temperature.

2.3 Resistor Combinations

Series Connection

Resistors connected end-to-end so the same current flows through each. Total voltage = sum of individual voltages. Equivalent resistance is always larger than the largest single resistor.

R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

Parallel Connection

Resistors share the same two nodes so the same voltage appears across each. Total current = sum of branch currents. Equivalent resistance is always smaller than the smallest single resistor.

1 / R_total = 1 / R₁ + 1 / R₂ + 1 / R₃ + ...

calculate Worked Example: Resistor Combination

Problem: Two 4 Ω resistors in parallel are connected in series with a 6 Ω resistor. Find the total resistance.

Solution:
1. Parallel part: 1/R_p = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2 → R_p = 2 Ω
2. Total: R_total = 2 + 6 = 8 Ω

2.4 Electromotive Force (EMF)

EMF (ℰ or E) is the energy supplied by a source per unit charge to drive charge around a complete circuit. It is the open-circuit voltage of the source. Despite the name, EMF is not a force — it is a potential difference measured in volts.

2.5 Internal Resistance

Every real cell has internal resistance r caused by the chemicals and electrodes inside it. When current flows, some voltage is lost inside the cell.

V = ℰ − I r     and     I = ℰ / (R + r)
★ MUST KNOW: Lost volts = I r (voltage dropped inside the cell). Terminal PD = I R (voltage across external load). ℰ = V + I r → EMF = Terminal PD + Lost volts.

calculate Worked Example: Internal Resistance

Problem: The open-circuit p.d. of a cell is 6 V. When 4 A is drawn the terminal voltage falls to 4 V. Find the internal resistance.

Solution:
ℰ = 6 V, V = 4 V, I = 4 A
Lost volts = ℰ − V = 2 V
r = (ℰ − V) / I = 2 / 4 = 0.5 Ω

2.6 Arrangement of Cells

Series Connection: Positive of one cell to negative of the next. Used for high voltage.
Total EMF = ℰ₁ + ℰ₂ + ... + ℰₙ | Total internal resistance = r₁ + r₂ + ... + rₙ
For n identical cells: ℰ_tot = nℰ, r_tot = n r

Parallel Connection: All positives joined, all negatives joined. Used for higher current capacity and longer life.
For identical cells: ℰ_eq = ℰ, r_eq = r / n

calculate Worked Example: Cells in Series

Problem: Five 1.5 V cells, each with r = 0.2 Ω, drive a 5 Ω load. Find the current.

Solution:
ℰ_tot = 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 V
r_tot = 5 × 0.2 = 1.0 Ω
I = ℰ_tot / (R + r_tot) = 7.5 / (5 + 1) = 1.25 A

2.7 Thermionic Emission

Thermionic emission is the process by which free electrons are emitted from the surface of a metal when external heat energy is applied. First observed by Thomas A. Edison in 1883. The emitted electrons are called thermions.

Rate of emission depends on: nature of material, surface temperature, surface area. A good emitter needs low work function, large surface area, and high melting point. Common materials: tungsten, thoriated tungsten, barium-oxide coated metals.

J = A T² exp(−eφ / kT)     (Richardson–Dushman equation)

Devices that use thermionic emission: vacuum tubes, cathode-ray tubes, X-ray tubes, diodes.

2.8 Diodes & Rectification

A diode is a two-terminal semiconductor device that conducts current primarily in one direction only. Low (or zero) resistance in the forward direction; high resistance in the reverse direction. Made from semiconductors (silicon or germanium). Contains an anode (+) and a cathode (−). Also called rectifiers because they convert AC to pulsating DC.

Rectification is the process of converting an alternating current (AC) signal into a direct current (DC) signal using diodes.
