📘 Module 6: Comparative External Morphology — Invertebrates

The Context: The Diversity of Animal Body Plans

Invertebrates make up over 95% of all animal species. They show an incredible range of body plans, from the simple sponge (which has no tissues or organs) to the complex arthropod (with jointed limbs and specialized body regions). Understanding their external morphology reveals how evolution has solved the same problems — feeding, movement, protection, reproduction — in vastly different ways.

Analogy: Invertebrates are like different models of cars. Some are simple (like a bicycle — Porifera), some are complex (like a sports car — Arthropoda), and some are specialized for specific environments (like a submarine — Mollusca). Each has a different body plan optimized for its way of life.

6.1 Porifera (Sponges)

  • Body covering: Pinacoderm (outer cell layer) perforated by ostia (pores); internal support from spicules or spongin fibers in a gelatinous mesohyl.
  • Limbs/appendages: None.
  • Head/sensory organs: None — no head, brain, or nervous system.
  • Locomotion: Sessile as adults; water flow driven by flagellated choanocytes.
  • Reproduction: Mostly hermaphroditic; internal fertilization via sperm entering through ostia; also reproduce asexually by budding or gemmules.
Key Feature: Choanocytes — specialized flagellated cells that create water currents for feeding and gas exchange. Sponges have no organs, only specialized cells working together.

6.2 Cnidaria (Hydra, Jellyfish)

  • Body covering: Delicate epidermis with stinging cnidocytes; two body forms — polyp (sessile) and medusa (free-swimming), some alternate between both.
  • Limbs/appendages: Tentacles around the mouth, used to paralyze and draw in prey.
  • Head/sensory organs: No true head; may have ocelli (light-detecting) and statocysts (balance) especially in medusae.
  • Locomotion: Medusae pulse the bell to swim; polyps mostly stationary, can contract/glide.
  • Reproduction: External fertilization via gametes released into water; polyps can also bud asexually.

6.3 Platyhelminthes (Flatworms)

  • Body covering: Soft, dorsoventrally flattened; ciliated epidermis (free-living) or non-ciliated tegument (parasitic); bilateral symmetry, cephalization begins here.
  • Limbs/appendages: None.
  • Head/sensory organs: Primitive eyespots (ocelli) and auricles (chemoreception).
  • Locomotion: Free-living forms glide via cilia + muscle contraction; parasitic forms use hooks/suckers.
  • Reproduction: Mostly hermaphroditic; free-living forms like Planaria show external genital pores and complex mating.
Evolutionary Significance: Flatworms mark a significant step toward cephalization — the concentration of sensory organs and nervous tissue at one end of the body, seen here for the first time via primitive eyespots and chemoreceptors in a defined head region.

6.4 Nematoda (Roundworms)

  • Body covering: Smooth, thick, non-living cuticle that is periodically molted.
  • Limbs/appendages: None.
  • Head/sensory organs: Mouth with lips; paired amphids (chemosensory pits).
  • Locomotion: Only longitudinal muscles — alternating contraction produces whip-like thrashing.
  • Reproduction: Mostly dioecious (separate sexes); males have copulatory spicules for internal fertilization.
Why Whip-like Movement? Nematodes only have longitudinal muscles (no circular muscles), which contract alternately on each side of the body. Without circular muscles, the result is a whip-like thrashing rather than the smoother wave-like crawling seen in annelids.

6.5 Annelida (Segmented Worms)

  • Body covering: Thin, moist, permeable cuticle with setae (bristles) for locomotion; body divided into repeated segments (metamerism).
  • Limbs/appendages: Parapodia (fleshy appendages) in some polychaetes; setae in others.
  • Head/sensory organs: Well-developed head in many species; eyes, tentacles, sensory palps.
  • Locomotion: Peristaltic movement using circular and longitudinal muscles; setae grip the substrate.
  • Reproduction: Mostly hermaphroditic (earthworms) or separate sexes; clitellum secretes a cocoon for eggs.
Metamerism: True segmentation — the body is divided into repeated internal and external segments. Annelids show this clearly: externally as ring-like annuli, each corresponding to internal compartments, enabling precise coordinated movement.

6.6 Arthropoda (Insects, Arachnids, Crustaceans)

  • Body covering: Thick, jointed chitinous exoskeleton, periodically molted (ecdysis).
  • Limbs/appendages: Jointed, highly specialized — insects: 6 legs; arachnids: 8; crustaceans: many legs + swimmers.
  • Head/sensory organs: Distinct head with compound eyes, antennae, specialized mouthparts.
  • Locomotion: Legs (walking), wings (flight in insects), swimmers/paddles (swimming in crustaceans).
  • Body segmentation: Organized into tagmata: head-thorax-abdomen (insects) or cephalothorax-abdomen (spiders, crustaceans).
  • Reproduction: Usually internal fertilization; modified appendages for mating (e.g., pedipalps in spiders).
Analogy: Arthropods are like Swiss Army knives — their exoskeleton and jointed limbs allow incredible versatility. One pair of appendages can be legs, jaws, antennae, or claws depending on the species and its needs.

6.7 Mollusca (Snails, Clams, Squids)

  • Body covering: Soft, unsegmented body; often a calcareous shell secreted by the mantle; bilateral symmetry.
  • Limbs/appendages: Muscular foot (snails, clams); modified into arms/tentacles with suckers in cephalopods.
  • Head/sensory organs: Well-developed in most classes except bivalves; cephalopods have highly developed camera-like eyes.
  • Locomotion: Gliding on mucus (snails), burrowing (bivalves), jet propulsion via siphon (cephalopods).
  • Reproduction: Internal gonads opening via ducts; cephalopod males use a modified arm (hectocotylus) to transfer sperm.

6.8 Echinodermata (Starfish, Sea Urchins)

  • Body covering: Tough, spiny/leathery skin over an internal endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles.
  • Limbs/appendages: Multiple arms/rays radiating from a central disc (not true limbs).
  • Head/sensory organs: No true head or brain; eyespots at arm tips detect light; tube feet are sensory.
  • Locomotion: Tube feet, part of the unique water vascular system (also used in feeding/respiration).
  • Reproduction: Mostly separate sexes; external fertilization.
Why They're Advanced: Despite lacking a head and jointed limbs, echinoderms are considered the most evolutionarily advanced invertebrates — due to deuterostome development, an internal skeleton, and a close evolutionary relationship to chordates.

6.9 Hemichordata (Acorn Worms)

  • Body covering: Soft, ciliated, mucus-producing skin; no hard shell or skeleton.
  • Limbs/appendages: None true; some pterobranchs have small tentacle-like arms.
  • Head/sensory organs: Body in three parts — proboscis, collar, trunk; no eyes, simple nerve networks only.
  • Locomotion: Ciliary beating and proboscis muscle contraction — burrowing/crawling.
  • Reproduction: Usually dioecious; gonopores release gametes into water; external fertilization.
Evolutionary Bridge: Hemichordates are deuterostomes, like echinoderms and chordates — their larvae resemble echinoderm larvae, and features like a rod-like stomach and dorsal nerve cord echo chordate traits. They act as an evolutionary "bridge" toward vertebrates.

📝 Module 6 Self-Assessment Test

Q1: A sponge (Porifera) has no head, brain, or nervous system, yet it can feed and exchange gases effectively. What structure enables this, and how does it work?
Q2: Compare the polyp and medusa body forms in Cnidaria — which is sessile, and which is free-swimming?
Q3: What evolutionary significance does the flatworm (Platyhelminthes) head region have?
Q4: Why does a nematode move with a 'whip-like thrashing motion' rather than smooth crawling?
Q5: What is metamerism, and which phylum covered here shows it clearly?
Q6: List the three tagmata (body regions) typical of insects, and the two typical of spiders/crustaceans.
Q7: Why are cephalopods (squids, octopuses) considered unusual among molluscs in terms of their sensory organs?
Q8: Echinoderms lack a head, brain, and jointed limbs, yet are considered the most evolutionarily advanced invertebrates. Why?
Q9: What makes hemichordates an interesting evolutionary 'bridge,' and what feature do they share with chordates?

✅ Full Solutions

A1: Choanocytes — specialized flagellated cells lining the interior. Their flagella beat to drive water currents through the pores (ostia) and out through the osculum, enabling feeding, gas exchange, and waste removal without any centralized nervous coordination.

A2: The polyp form (e.g., Hydra) is tubular and typically sessile/stationary. The medusa form (e.g., jellyfish) is bell-shaped and free-swimming, moving via rhythmic pulsation of the bell. Some cnidarian species alternate between both forms across their life cycle.

A3: It marks a significant step toward cephalization — the concentration of sensory organs and nervous tissue at one end of the body, seen here for the first time via primitive eyespots (ocelli) and auricles (chemoreceptors) in a defined head region.

A4: Because nematodes only have longitudinal muscles (no circular muscles), which contract alternately on each side of the body. Without circular muscles to complement them, the result is a whip-like thrashing rather than the smoother wave-like crawling seen in annelids, which have both muscle types.

A5: Metamerism is true segmentation — the body is divided into repeated internal and external segments. Annelida (segmented worms) shows this clearly: externally as ring-like annuli, each corresponding to internal compartments, enabling precise coordinated movement.

A6: Insects: head, thorax, abdomen. Spiders and crustaceans: cephalothorax (fused head + thorax) and abdomen.

A7: Because they possess highly developed eyes comparable in complexity to vertebrate eyes — unlike most other molluscs (e.g., bivalves, which lack a well-developed head/sensory region entirely).

A8: Because of their deuterostome development, internal endoskeleton (calcareous ossicles), and close evolutionary relationship to chordates — making them a key transitional group on the evolutionary path toward vertebrates.

A9: They are deuterostomes whose larvae closely resemble echinoderm larvae (hinting at shared ancestry), while adult features like a rod-like stomach and dorsal nerve cord echo traits seen in chordates — making them a link helping explain how the deuterostome lineage gave rise to chordates and eventually vertebrates.
