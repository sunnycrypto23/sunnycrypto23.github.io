Module 1: Forces in Nature & Electrostatics

The complete, in-depth lecture notes for PHY 102 Module 1. Extracted from the official course PDF.

1. Introduction to Electromagnetism

During your first year studying physics in the university, you will study electric force and magnetic force which occur in nature because of the fact that the constituents of matter have electric charges. The study of electromagnetism adds something new to the ideas of mechanics, namely electric and magnetic fields.

These entities are just as real as masses and forces and they take center stage when we discuss the phenomenon of electromagnetic radiation, a topic which includes the behavior of visible light.

The entire picture of matter and fields that you will acquire at the end of this course is known as classical physics. This picture, which is complete enough for many fields of engineering, is not a complete statement of the laws of nature. New phenomena discovered in the early 20th century demanded revisions in our thinking about the relation of space and time (relativity) and about phenomena on the atomic scale (quantum physics).

2. The Four Fundamental Forces in Nature

Every force we encounter in nature must be due to one of these four fundamental forces:

1. Gravitational Force

Gravitational force is the force between any two bodies due to their mass. It is the force responsible for keeping us attached to the earth's surface. Gravitational force is always attractive and is the weakest of the four forces.

2. Electromagnetic Force

Electromagnetic force is the force responsible for the attraction and repulsion of electric charges and is also responsible for the magnetic force. Besides gravity, most forces encountered in everyday life are electromagnetic in nature. For example, when you push on something with your hand, you are not really in direct contact with it: the outermost electrons of the atoms at the surface of the object are being electrically repelled by the outermost electrons in the atoms at the surface of your hand.

3. Strong Nuclear Force

Strong nuclear force is the force that holds together protons and neutrons in the atomic nucleus. It overpowers the electromagnetic mutual repulsion of the protons in the nucleus. It is the force responsible for nuclear fusion, which is the process that causes the sun to shine.

4. Weak Nuclear Force

Weak nuclear force is responsible for the process of Beta decay in which a neutron in the atomic nucleus decays into a proton and an electron. While the proton remains in the nucleus, the electron escapes from the atom.

3. Electrostatics & Electric Charge

Electrostatics is the branch of physics that deals with the studies of static or slow-moving electric charges and the forces they exert. These forces are described by Coulomb's law.

Electric charge is a fundamental physical quantity of matter that causes it to experience a force when placed in an electric field. The two kinds of electric charges are positive and negative charge. The negative charge is the charge possessed by electrons and positive charge is the one possessed by protons.

Elementary Charge: e = 1.602177 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Key Properties of Electric Charge

  1. Quantization: Electric charge exists in discrete units, the smallest being the elementary charge (e). Any bigger charge is always an integer multiple of this elementary charge: q = ne (where n = 0, ±1, ±2...).
  2. Conservation: The total electric charge in an isolated system remains constant. Charge cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between objects.
  3. Attraction and Repulsion: Like charges (positive-positive or negative-negative) repel each other, while unlike charges (positive-negative) attract.
  4. Additivity: Total charge of a system is the algebraic sum of all individual charges present.

4. Charging Methods

Observation shows that objects can be charged in three distinct ways:

  • Friction: Transferring electrons between materials by rubbing them together.
  • Conduction: Transferring charge by direct contact between charged and uncharged objects.
  • Induction: Separating charge within an object by bringing a charged object nearby, without direct contact.

5. Worked Examples from the Module

calculate Example 1: Total Charge of Electrons

Problem: What is the total charge of 75.0 kg of electrons?

Solution:

The mass of an electron is mₑ = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg. The number of electrons (N) in mass M = 75.0 kg is:

N = M / m = 75.0 / (9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 8.23 × 10³¹ electrons

The charge of 1 electron is -e = -1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. The total charge (Q) is:

Q = N(-e) = (8.23 × 10³¹)(-1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) = -1.32 × 10¹³ C

calculate Example 2: The Penny Problem

Problem: A penny (3.11g of copper) is given a charge of +1.0 × 10⁻⁷ C. What fraction of its electrons were removed? (Copper has 29 electrons per atom, atomic weight 63.54 g/mol).

Solution:

First, find the number of electrons removed (N):

N = q_total / q_e = (1.0 × 10⁻⁷) / (1.60 × 10¹⁹) = 6.2 × 10¹¹ electrons

Next, find the total number of electrons in a neutral penny. First, find moles of copper:

n_Cu = 3.11g / 63.54g/mol = 4.89 × 10⁻² mol

Number of copper atoms (N_Cu):

N_Cu = n_Cu × N_A = (4.89 × 10⁻²)(6.022 × 10²³) = 2.95 × 10²² atoms

Total electrons originally in the penny (N_e):

N_e = 29 × N_Cu = 29(2.95 × 10²²) = 8.55 × 10²³ electrons

Finally, the fraction (f) of electrons removed:

f = (6.2 × 10¹¹) / (8.55 × 10²³) = 7.3 × 10⁻¹³

This is an incredibly tiny fraction!
