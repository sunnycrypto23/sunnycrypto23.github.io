Unit 3: Magnetism & Magnetic Fields

The complete, unsummarized lecture notes for PHY 102 Unit 3, extracted directly from the official Comprehensive Handbook.

3.0 Concept of Magnetic Field

A magnetic field is a region of space where a magnetic force can be detected. It is produced by moving electric charges (currents) or by intrinsic magnetic moments of elementary particles (like electrons). The magnetic field is a vector quantity, denoted by B.

Magnetic Field Lines: Imaginary lines used to visualize the magnetic field. They emerge from the North pole and enter the South pole. They never intersect. The density of the lines indicates the strength of the field.

3.1 Magnetic Flux & Flux Density

Magnetic Flux (Φ): The total number of magnetic field lines passing through a given surface area. It is a scalar quantity. SI unit: Weber (Wb).

Φ = B · A = B A cosθ

Where θ is the angle between the magnetic field vector B and the normal (perpendicular) to the surface area A. Maximum flux when θ = 0° (field perpendicular to surface). Zero flux when θ = 90° (field parallel to surface).

Magnetic Flux Density (B): The magnetic flux per unit area perpendicular to the field. It is the strength of the magnetic field. SI unit: Tesla (T). 1 T = 1 Wb/m². Another common unit is Gauss (G): 1 T = 10,000 G.

3.2 The Lorentz Force

The Lorentz force is the total force experienced by a charged particle moving through both an electric field E and a magnetic field B.

F = q(E + v × B)

If there is no electric field (E = 0), the magnetic force is:

F = q v B sinθ

Where θ is the angle between the velocity vector v and the magnetic field vector B. The direction of the magnetic force is given by the Right-Hand Rule (for positive charges) or Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.

★ MUST KNOW: The magnetic force is always perpendicular to both the velocity and the magnetic field. Because F v, the magnetic force does NO WORK on the moving charge. It changes the direction of the charge, but not its speed or kinetic energy.

3.3 Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field experiences a force because the moving charges (electrons) inside the wire experience a Lorentz force.

F = B I L sinθ

Where I is the current, L is the length of the wire in the field, and θ is the angle between the current direction and the magnetic field. Maximum force when the wire is perpendicular to the field (θ = 90°). Zero force when the wire is parallel to the field (θ = 0°).

calculate Worked Example: Force on a Wire

Problem: A straight wire of length 0.5 m carries a current of 4 A. It is placed in a uniform magnetic field of 0.2 T at an angle of 30° to the field. Find the magnetic force on the wire.

Solution:
F = B I L sinθ = (0.2)(4)(0.5) sin(30°) = 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2 N

3.4 Biot-Savart Law

The Biot-Savart law calculates the magnetic field dB produced by a tiny segment of current-carrying wire dl at a distance r.

dB = (μ₀ / 4π) × (I dl sinθ / r²)

Where μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A is the permeability of free space. It is the magnetic equivalent of Coulomb's law for electric fields.

Magnetic field at the center of a circular loop of radius R:

B = μ₀ I / (2R)

3.5 Ampère’s Circuital Law

Ampère’s law states that the line integral of the magnetic field B around any closed loop is equal to μ₀ times the total current enclosed by the loop. It is highly useful for calculating B in situations with high symmetry.

∮ B · dl = μ₀ I_enc

3.6 Magnetic Field of a Solenoid

A solenoid is a long coil of wire wound in a tight helix. Inside an ideal (infinitely long) solenoid, the magnetic field is uniform, strong, and parallel to the axis. Outside, the field is approximately zero.

B = μ₀ n I

Where n = N/L is the number of turns per unit length. The field depends only on the current and the turn density, not on the radius of the solenoid.

calculate Worked Example: Solenoid Field

Problem: A solenoid has 1000 turns per meter and carries a current of 2 A. Calculate the magnetic field inside the solenoid.

Solution:
B = μ₀ n I = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(1000)(2) = 8π × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 2.51 × 10⁻³ T (or 2.51 mT)

3.7 Magnetic Dipoles & Torque

A current loop acts as a magnetic dipole. The magnetic dipole moment μ is defined as:

μ = N I A

Where N is the number of turns, I is the current, and A is the area of the loop. The direction of μ is given by the right-hand rule (curl fingers in direction of current, thumb points in direction of μ).

When placed in a uniform magnetic field, the loop experiences a torque τ that tries to align μ with B:

τ = μ × B    |    τ = N I A B sinθ

This is the fundamental principle behind electric motors and galvanometers.

3.8 Dielectrics & Polarization

A dielectric is an insulating material that can be polarized by an applied electric field. When placed in an electric field, the positive and negative charges within the dielectric shift slightly, creating an internal electric field that opposes the external field.

Dielectric Constant (κ): The ratio of the permittivity of the material to the permittivity of free space. κ = ε / ε₀. It is always > 1. For vacuum, κ = 1.

ε = κ ε₀

Inserting a dielectric between the plates of a capacitor increases its capacitance by a factor of κ: C_new = κ C_old. It also increases the maximum voltage the capacitor can withstand before breaking down (dielectric strength).
